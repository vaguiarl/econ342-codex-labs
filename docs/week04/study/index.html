Gravity in three ideas

Comparative advantage helps explain what countries trade. Gravity helps explain with whom—and how much.

  1. Large markets pull trade up.
  2. Distance and other costs push trade down.
  3. A fitted value is a benchmark, not a causal effect.

Compare ratios, then interpret

Partner B is four times as large as partner A and twice as far. Let both exponents equal 1.

predicted trade ratio = 4 ÷ 2 = 2

Answer

2×

Canada is predicted to export twice as much to B as to A. Market size more than offsets distance.

Check If B were twice as large and twice as far, the ratio would be 2 ÷ 2 = 1: the forces exactly offset.

A doubling is a power calculation

The PPML distance coefficient is −0.666.

  1. Compute 2−0.666 = 0.630.
  2. Predicted trade becomes 63.0% of its old level.
  3. 100(1 − 0.630) = 37.0% less.

Complete interpretation

37% less

A pair twice as far apart is predicted to trade about 37% less, holding the included variables fixed.

Say “is associated with”, not “distance causes,” because this cross-section is descriptive.

Try first—then reveal the answer

1. What are gravity’s two forces, and why does Canada trade heavily with the U.S.?

Market size pulls trade up; distance and other trade costs push it down. The U.S. is large, adjacent, shares a border with Canada, and is connected through long-standing supply chains.

2. If the distance coefficient is −0.666, what happens when distance doubles?

2−0.666 = 0.630. Predicted trade is 63.0% as large, or 37.0% lower, holding the included variables fixed.

3. What does 14.2 ÷ 4.1 = 3.46 mean?

Observed Canadian export orientation toward the U.S. was 3.46 times the model’s fitted orientation. The residual is not a causal CUSMA estimate because it also contains omitted products, firms, history, networks, policy, and measurement.
