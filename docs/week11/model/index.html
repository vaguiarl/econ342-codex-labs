Follow the mechanism

  1. Delivered costs fall on shorter routes. Fewer goods are used up in shipping, making some suppliers more attractive.
  2. Purchases and wages adjust together. Each region spends its income; wages adjust until spending on its goods matches that income.
  3. Prices and income determine purchasing power. A lower basket price can more than offset a lower nominal wage.
Open the assumptions and model equations

Illustrative inputs: fixed labour of 2, 1, and 3 units in Atlantic US, Pacific US, and Pacific Asia; equal productivity of 1; common tastes. Each region produces a distinct good under constant returns.

Shipping: delivered unit cost is the origin wage × shipping factor. Between regions, shipping factor = 1 + shipping parameter × distance / 10,000. Within a region it is 1. Shipping uses resources; this model has no toll revenue.

Purchases: buyers choose among three regional goods with a constant-elasticity demand system. The substitution elasticity is θ + 1; a supplier’s spending weight is its delivered cost raised to −θ, divided by the sum of all suppliers’ weights.

Equilibrium: each regional budget equals wage × fixed labour. Each supplier’s total sales equal that budget. World nominal income is normalized to 6 in both states; only relative prices and purchasing power matter.

Held fixed: labour location, productivity, preferences, and the three-region structure. No migration, firm entry, increasing returns, or Krugman scale mechanism is simulated. There are no investment costs, congestion, war shocks, or historical calibration.

Historical inputs: the two New York distance comparisons come from Marshall’s 1913 map. The 4,600-nautical-mile San Francisco–Yokohama link is an assumption. Route arithmetic is separate from the model’s economic predictions.
