Which firms export?

Firms in the same industry differ. Fixed export costs select the most productive firms into foreign markets and turn trade into a process of entry, expansion, contraction, and exit.

Five facts any firm-level trade model must explain

  1. Only a minority of firms export.
  2. A small group of exporters accounts for most export value.
  3. Exporters tend to be larger and more productive and often pay more.
  4. Many firms enter and leave export markets after short spells.
  5. Trade changes market shares and survival within the same industry.

Model test Which fact is hardest for a model in which every firm is identical?

An identical-firm model misses the central facts

Week 6: symmetric firms

Every variety has the same productivity and cost. The model explains scale, variety, and two-way trade.

Week 7: heterogeneous firms

Firms draw different productivity. Fixed operating and export costs create different decisions inside one industry.

The extension: keep differentiated varieties and fixed costs, then allow productivity to differ across firms.

Conceptual source: Melitz (2003), The Impact of Trade on Intra-Industry Reallocations and Aggregate Industry Productivity.

The heterogeneous-firm model in one paragraph

A prospective firm pays to enter and discovers how productive it is. Higher productivity means lower unit cost. Every active firm must cover a domestic operating cost; exporting adds market research, compliance, distribution, customs, and relationship costs. The weakest firms exit, a middle group serves only Canada, and the most productive group also exports.

Exitcannot cover domestic fixed cost
Domestic onlycovers Canada, not export cost
Exportercovers both fixed costs

Scope: our linear-demand version makes productive firms choose larger quantities and shows selection. Churn over time still needs changing demand, costs, or productivity, possibly interacting with sunk costs.

The firm chooses each quantity. Export profit is incremental: an exporter must first find domestic operation worthwhile.

One derivative delivers the optimal quantity

Write inverse demand as p(q) = A − q/2, delivered marginal cost as m, and the fixed cost as F.

π(q) = (A − q/2 − m)q − F

First-order condition

π′(q) = A − q − m = 0

Therefore q* = A − m. Since π″(q) = −1, this is the unique maximum.

Profit at the optimum

p(q*) − m = q*/2
π* = (q*)²/2 − F

A fixed cost changes entry, not q* conditional on entry.

A firm may pay to enter before knowing its productivity

Suppose Spruce, Maple, and Cedar productivity types are equally likely. A failed type exits and earns zero after observing its draw.

Expected operating profit = (0 + 42 + 200) ÷ 3 = 80.7

If the sunk entry cost is 75, expected net profit is 5.7 and entry is worthwhile.

If the sunk entry cost is 90, expected net profit is −9.3 and the firm stays out.

Free entry does not mean costless entry. It means potential entrants compare a sunk cost with uncertain expected profit.

“Exit” can mean four different things

Business entry

A new firm begins operating.

Export entry

An existing firm begins selling abroad.

Export exit

A firm stops selling abroad but may remain active in Canada.

Firm exit

The business ceases operating.

The export-survival curve tracks export spells. The business-dynamism series tracks operating firms. Combining the two without checking definitions creates false conclusions.

Selection and learning can coexist

Selection

Exporters often differ before entry: more productive plants are already more likely to enter exporting. Evidence that the advantage predates export entry supports this channel.

Learning and investment

Some Canadian plants induced to export after U.S. tariff cuts adopted advanced technology, innovated, and raised productivity.

+15.3%labour-productivity increase among FTA-induced new exporters in Lileeva–Trefler’s Canadian estimate

Do not turn a heterogeneous effect into “exporting raises every firm’s productivity by 15.3%.”

Source: Lileeva and Trefler (2010), Improved Access to Foreign Markets Raises Plant-level Productivity…For Some Plants.

Firms add destinations one fixed cost at a time

85.7%of Canadian goods exporters sold to the United States, 2024
65.9%sold exclusively to the United States
34.1%sold to at least one non-U.S. destination

A nearby, large, familiar market has lower delivered and fixed entry costs. More firms can clear the U.S. cutoff than a distant-market cutoff. Gravity therefore appears at the firm level as a destination ladder.

Source: Statistics Canada, Trade in goods by exporter characteristics, 2024.

Different trade costs act on different margins

Fixed export costs

Certification, legal setup, market research, distributor search, and customs systems. Lowering them mainly helps firms begin serving a market.

Per-unit trade costs

Freight, tariffs, delays, and border paperwork tied to each shipment. Lowering them raises sales and can also bring marginal firms across the cutoff.

Policy judgment Which instrument would better help a capable small firm that cannot afford its first foreign certification?

What to remember

  1. Most firms do not export, and export value is extremely concentrated.
  2. Higher productivity lowers unit cost and helps firms cover fixed market-entry costs.
  3. Two cutoffs separate exit, domestic-only production, and exporting.
  4. Trade can raise industry productivity through reallocation, even without learning.
  5. Export entry and firm entry are different, as are export exit and firm death.

A five-step recipe for a linear-demand firm problem

  1. Compute costs: unit cost c = w/a; delivered marginal cost m = c + t.
  2. Write the problem: p(q) = A − bq and π(q) = [p(q) − m]q − F.
  3. Optimize: differentiate, set π′(q) = 0, and check π″(q) < 0.
  4. Plug back: after finding q*, use inverse demand: p* = p(q*) = A − bq*. Then compute π*.
  5. Make the decision: enter or export if and only if π* ≥ 0.

Symbolic result

π′(q) = A − 2bq − m
q* = (A − m) ÷ (2b)

MR determines the optimal quantity; demand determines price. With b > 0, π″(q) = −2b < 0.

Classroom shortcut

b = 1/2  ⇒  q* = A − m

Keep the two decisions separate: F is absent from the first-order condition. It changes entry, not q* conditional on entry.
