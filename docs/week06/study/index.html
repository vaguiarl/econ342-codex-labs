Economies of scale in three ideas

A larger accessible market can support longer production runs, lower fixed cost per unit, and trade in differentiated varieties.

  1. Fixed cost makes average cost fall with output.
  2. People value different varieties of similar goods.
  3. Countries specialize in varieties and trade both ways.

Measure how balanced two-way trade is

GL = 1 − |X − M| ÷ (X + M)
1 − |67.791 − 72.973| ÷ 140.764 = 0.963

Canada–U.S. auto trade is highly balanced in the two directions.

GL = 1: exports equal imports.

GL = 0: trade is entirely one-way.

A high value is descriptive evidence—not proof of a particular cause.

Memory rule: the smaller the imbalance relative to total trade, the closer GL is to one.

A smaller production run means higher fixed cost per unit

Illustration: F = C$600M, c = C$30k, integrated output = 150,000 vehicles.

AC = 30 + 600 ÷ q

Integrated market: q = 150k → AC = C$34.0k.

Remove 76.4% mechanically: q = 35.4k → AC = C$46.9k.

+38.1%illustrated increase in average cost

Not a forecast. Firms could redirect sales, change prices or inputs, resize capacity, or close. The 76.4% demand share is not a causal tariff effect.

Lesson: access to demand matters because it determines how many units can share the fixed cost.

Fixed cost creates economies of scale

AC(q) = c + F ÷ q

F: fixed cost

Plant, design, engineering, testing, certification.

c: marginal cost

Labour, tires, steel, glass, batteries for one more unit.

q: output

The number of units sharing the fixed cost.

Mechanism: as q rises, F/q falls. Average cost approaches—but does not fall below—marginal cost.

Specialize by model—not simply by “cars”

Canada

Produces selected vehicles and parts in longer runs, then exports them to the larger North American market.

Partners

Produce other models and stages in longer runs, which Canada imports because buyers value variety.

specialization by variety + preference for choice = exports and imports within one industry

Examples of varieties: pickups, minivans, compact cars, SUVs, delivery vans, luxury crossovers.

Comparative advantage and scale are complements

Comparative advantage

Explains specialization across goods or stages using opportunity cost.

Canadian clue: crude oil and aluminum have low GL.

Scale and variety

Explains specialization among differentiated products with fixed cost.

Canadian clue: autos, machinery, aerospace, chemicals have high GL.

Try first—then reveal the answer

1. Why does average cost fall when output rises?

Because AC = c + F/q. Marginal cost stays at c, while more units share the fixed cost, so F/q falls.

2. What does the auto GL value of 0.963 mean?

Canada’s exports and imports of vehicles and parts with the United States were very similar in size. About 96.3% of gross trade remains after subtracting the imbalance. It does not prove scale is the only cause.

3. Why can Canada export and import cars simultaneously?

Firms specialize in different vehicle models and stages, produce longer runs for a continental market, export those varieties, and import other varieties made at scale elsewhere.

Ready when you can: state the mechanism, show the simple arithmetic, cite one Canadian fact, and add one caveat.
