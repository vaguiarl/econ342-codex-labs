The Krugman trade model in four ideas

The model explains why similar countries export and import different varieties within the same industry.

1 · Variety: consumers value differentiated products.

2 · Scale: every variety requires a fixed cost.

3 · Competition: substitutes and entry limit pricing power.

4 · Trade: countries specialize by variety.

Measure how balanced two-way trade is

GL = 1 − |X − M| ÷ (X + M)
1 − |67.791 − 72.973| ÷ 140.764 = 0.963

Canada–U.S. auto trade is highly balanced in the two directions.

GL = 1: exports equal imports.

GL = 0: trade is entirely one-way.

A high value is descriptive evidence—not proof of a particular cause.

Memory rule: the smaller the imbalance relative to total trade, the closer GL is to one.

Why similar countries trade similar products

People value different varieties. Each variety requires a fixed cost. A firm has some pricing power because its product differs from others, but close substitutes and entry limit profit. Trade lets countries specialize in different varieties, export some, and import others.

“Monopolistic”: one firm controls its exact variety and faces downward-sloping demand.

“Competition”: close substitutes and entry prevent persistent excess profit.

Sources: Krugman, 1979 and 1980.

Fixed cost creates economies of scale

AC(q) = c + F ÷ q

F: fixed cost

Plant, design, engineering, testing, certification.

c: marginal cost

Labour, tires, steel, glass, batteries for one more unit.

q: output

The number of units sharing the fixed cost.

Mechanism: as q rises, F/q falls. Average cost approaches—but does not fall below—marginal cost.

Contribution per vehicle:

C$600M ÷ C$4k = 150,000

The firm must sell enough units for its total contribution margin to cover the fixed cost.

Trade can double available varieties without changing technology

Suppose each identical country demands 600,000 vehicles and a variety needs 150,000 sales to break even.

4varieties available in each separate market
8varieties available after market integration

Canada produces four and imports four; the United States does the same. Trade is two-way even though the countries have identical technology.

Key nuance: in this clean long-run example, firm output remains 150,000. The gain comes from more variety. Larger surviving plants are an additional rationalization channel to test in the data.

Try first, then reveal the answer

1. Why can identical countries trade within one industry?

Consumers value differentiated varieties and every variety has a fixed cost. Firms specialize in subsets of varieties, export those products, and import varieties produced elsewhere. Comparative advantage is not required for this two-way trade.

2. How do fixed cost and break-even sales fit together?

Average cost is c + F/q. Each sale contributes p − c toward the fixed cost, so break-even output is F/(p − c).

3. What does each model add to Canada–U.S. auto trade?

Ricardo helps explain which models, parts, or stages locate in Canada. Krugman explains two-way variety trade. Gravity explains why the large and nearby United States is Canada’s dominant partner.
