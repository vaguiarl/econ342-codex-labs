Compare opportunity costs, check the trade price, and treat export data as evidence—not proof of a cause.

Try first—then reveal the answer

1. Why can a country with no absolute advantage still export something?

Trade direction depends on comparative advantage. A country exports the good with its lower opportunity cost, even if it uses more resources per unit in both goods.

2. Canada’s cost of a machine is 4 lumber and the U.S. cost is 3. Does a price of 3.5 work?

Yes. The U.S. receives 3.5 lumber for something that costs it 3; Canada pays 3.5 instead of giving up 4. The strict test is 3 < 3.5 < 4.

3. Fertilizer RS = 6.19. What does that mean—and not mean?

Fertilizers occupy 6.19 times the share of Canada’s merchandise-export basket that they occupy in the U.S. basket. It does not mean Canada exports 6.19 times as many dollars, nor does it prove technology causes the pattern.

4. What does a PPF slope of −¼ mean?

With lumber on the horizontal axis, one additional lumber costs ¼ unit of machinery.
