Why does Canada export some products and import others?

Comparative advantage is about relative opportunity cost—not who is most productive in absolute terms.

Model: Ricardo’s two-country logic

Tools: opportunity cost, PPFs, trade prices

Evidence: Canada and the United States

Supported by the chart

  • Fertilizers and wood occupy larger shares of Canada’s export basket.

Requires more evidence

  • Production technologies and factor endowments.
  • Distance, policy, scale, firms, and supply chains.
  • Whether a particular trade flow raises welfare.

Say it Absolute advantage compares levels. Comparative advantage compares opportunity costs.

Ricardo’s model predicts allocation from relative cost

What the model needs

  • Different relative production costs.
  • A trade price acceptable to both sides.
  • Resources that can move between the two activities.

What it does not need

  • Equal productivity levels.
  • One country to be absolutely best at a good.
  • Observed exports to prove the technology.

Prediction: a country tends to export the good for which its opportunity cost is lower.

Twenty-four labour hours produce linear PPFs

Canada: M = 3 − ¼L
U.S.: M = 8 − ⅓L

Intercepts show maximum output. Slopes show opportunity cost.

Reveal comparative advantage

A has comparative advantage in wheat; B has comparative advantage in software.

Reveal the price test

4 < 4.5 < 5, so B can sell software above its domestic cost while A buys below its domestic cost.

Revealed specialization compares export-basket shares

RSi = (Canada product i exports ÷ all Canada exports) ÷ (U.S. product i exports ÷ all U.S. exports)

How to read it

  • RS > 1: more prominent in Canada’s basket.
  • RS < 1: more prominent in the U.S. basket.
  • RS = 1: equal basket shares.

Observed specialization reflects several mechanisms

  • Productivity and resources
  • Distance and infrastructure
  • Tariffs and institutions
  • Scale, firms, and networks
  • Prices and foreign demand
→

What we observe

Export shares and bilateral trade

The data combine every mechanism at once. Ricardo isolates only the relative-production-cost channel.

Inference rule: say a pattern is “consistent with” comparative advantage unless you directly observe or identify the underlying relative costs.
