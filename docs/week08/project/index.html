Choose one

Two distributional questions

Both options require a defensible incidence finding, a serious contrary case, a sensitivity check, and a conditional policy conclusion.

Option A

Who won and who lost from the tariff shock?

Decision: Choose either a cross-sector scan or a deep study of one clearly defined sector. Identify who was hurt, protected, or able to adapt across Canada and the United States under protectionist measures in force or implemented from January 1, 2025 through October 31, 2026. A rigorous result may find no clear net winner.

Mental model

H–O and Stolper–Samuelson connect goods prices to factor returns. Use official evidence—not an additional model—to measure how ownership, slow worker and capital adjustment, supplier links, and geography concentrate gains or losses. Downstream users and consumers prevent “protected” from meaning “better off.”

Choose one equally rigorous scope

  • Cross-sector scan: compare 8–12 sectors; identify three documented losses and three protected or successfully adapted candidates.
  • One-sector deep dive: define an HS/NAICS boundary, trace the Canada–U.S. value chain, and rank incidence across at least three stakeholder groups. Alcoholic beverages and motor vehicles/parts are examples only; any policy-exposed sector with adequate open data is eligible.

Required evidence

  • A frozen tariff register with direction, rate, date, status, exemptions, and HS items.
  • A pre-policy benchmark and a transparent exposure measure: comparable across sectors for the scan, or mapped through the chosen value chain for the deep dive.
  • An outcome exhibit with policy dates, a defensible comparison, and a labour, input-cost, downstream-price, or consumer-price cross-check.
  • An incidence table with mechanisms, confidence, alternative explanations, and limits to generalizing beyond the chosen scope. A deep dive is not a study of one company.

Option B

Does trade raise welfare for every Canadian?

Decision: Define welfare and the population whose welfare matters, then assess the aggregate claim and the stronger universal claim separately. Every project must build a labour-income/employment and consumption-basket foundation; it may add one or two measurable non-income dimensions. Recommend one targeted adjustment policy.

Mental model

Trade changes labour income through export opportunity, import competition, productivity, and reallocation. It changes purchasing power through prices and variety. The same household can gain as a consumer and lose as a worker; other measurable dimensions can strengthen or reverse that conclusion.

Required evidence

  • A pre-specified welfare definition, population, time horizon, and direction of improvement.
  • The required material core: four worker groups, all five income-quintile baskets matched to prices, and at least four profiles combining earnings, employment risk, and consumption effects.
  • Up to two added dimensions—such as job security, time use, health, access, environment, or regional resilience—with the affected population, unit, direction, trade mechanism, horizon, and open data stated.
  • Report dimensions separately unless the weights used to combine them are defended; do not invent an arbitrary welfare index.
  • A 2%, 5%, and 8% price scenario, break-even earnings loss, and sensitivity for the wage or earnings response. If welfare dimensions conflict, an ambiguous result under unspecified social weights can be correct.

Four-page discipline

Each page has one job

Lead with the finding. Background belongs only where it helps the reader evaluate the decision.

Finding and scope

Define groups, dates, baseline, comparison, answer, and confidence in the first substantive paragraph.

Mechanism

Trace the shock through the model to named sectors, factors, workers, consumers, or households.

Evidence and incidence

Establish magnitudes and a defensible incidence finding. State what the exhibits do and do not identify.

Decision and limits

Confront contrary evidence, uncertainty, distribution, policy implication, next check, and reversal condition.

One-week Codex workflow

Use AI like a research assistant

AI can inspect files, build concordances, test code, and red-team a draft. It cannot become a source or certify its own work.

1 · Freeze scope

Declare groups, period, policy status, comparison, incidence rule, and exclusions.

2 · Acquire

Save official raw files unchanged and record definitions, dates, units, and revisions.

3 · Reconcile

Audit HS, NAICS, CPI, spending, and labour-data mappings before calculation.

4 · Measure

Generate two exhibits and rerun the result under a meaningful alternative assumption.

5 · Draft

Write the answer before the background and connect every exhibit to the decision.

6 · Red-team

Search for direction errors, fragile incidence findings, unsupported causality, and ignored contrary evidence.

7 · Reproduce

Run from raw data in a clean folder; verify observations, links, sources, and AI corrections.

Hard gates

  • No verified open data or reproducible code: overall maximum 50.
  • Does not answer the exact question: first row 0 and overall maximum 50.
  • Theory named but not mapped to incidence: mechanism maximum level 2.
  • Exposure or correlation presented as causal: design maximum level 2.
  • No meaningful sensitivity: evidence maximum level 3.
  • Fabricated or untraceable evidence: evidence and reproducibility receive 0.

Submit one auditable package per pair.

PDF: exactly four narrative pages, at most one references/data page, and at most one reproducibility/AI-use page. ZIP: README, unchanged raw data, processed data, code, generated figures, source audit, verification report, AI log, and contributions.

The final test: could a skeptical policy reader reproduce the exhibits, understand exactly who gains and loses, see the best evidence against the finding, and know what would change the decision?
