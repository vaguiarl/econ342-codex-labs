Why Heckscher–Ohlin matters

Ricardo: relative costs and gains

Comparative advantage says that a country tends to export goods with a lower opportunity cost. Specialization and exchange can raise total real income.

What Ricardo leaves open: why do relative costs differ across countries, and who owns the resources that become more or less valuable after trade?

H–O: endowments and ownership

Countries have different relative supplies of productive factors, and goods use those factors in different proportions. This gives comparative advantage an endowment-based source.

The distribution extension: people earn wages and rents from factors. Stolper–Samuelson connects goods prices to the real incomes of factor owners.

Why we care: H–O supplies one source of comparative advantage. Paired with Stolper–Samuelson, it identifies potential long-run winners and losers.

Heckscher–Ohlin in plain English

Why endowments can shape trade

  1. Countries differ in their relative supplies of land, labour, capital, resources, and skills.
  2. A relatively plentiful factor usually has a lower relative price.
  3. Goods use factors in different proportions. Goods that intensively use the abundant factor tend to have a lower relative cost.
  4. The country tends to export those goods and import goods that intensively use its scarce factors.

Canada example

Canada has abundant land and natural-resource assets relative to its population. The model therefore predicts relatively strong exports of cereals, lumber, fertilizers, ores, and energy.

The income link: people own factors. Export demand raises the return to the factor used intensively in the export good. Stolper–Samuelson formalizes this long-run income effect.

H–O does not explain every Canadian export

Close fit

  • Potash and ores use mineral deposits.
  • Cereals use land and transport infrastructure.
  • Forestry uses forests and mills.
  • Some services use a highly educated workforce.

Other mechanisms matter

  • Two-way auto trade reflects scale and supply chains.
  • Aerospace reflects clusters and accumulated capabilities.
  • Only some firms export within each industry.
  • Distance and market size shape every flow.

Observed trade combines endowments, technology, scale, firms, geography, history, and policy.

Openness and tariffs push factor incomes in opposite directions

More openness in the land-abundant example

World demand raises the relative price of grain. Grain uses land intensively, so land rent rises while the wage falls in real terms in the long-run model.

Tariff on labour-intensive apparel

The tariff raises the domestic price of apparel. Apparel uses labour intensively, so the wage rises and land rent falls, but every apparel buyer faces a higher price.

Trade policy changes domestic relative prices. Relative prices change factor demand. Factor demand changes real wages and rents.

Inequality link: a larger gap between land income and labour income can widen household inequality when land ownership is concentrated. Ownership, consumption, and taxes or transfers determine the final household result.

Stolper–Samuelson turns goods prices into real factor incomes

Export-good price rises

The real return to the factor used intensively in the export good rises. In the two-good model, it rises relative to both goods.

Other factor

The real return to the other factor falls. Cheaper imports do not guarantee that its owners gain overall.

Political implication: owners of relatively scarce factors can oppose openness even when national real income rises.

Scope: the exact theorem assumes two goods, two factors, common technology, competitive markets, and both goods produced. Use it as disciplined intuition rather than a Canadian point forecast.

Use H–O to predict incidence—then test what it omits

What the model predicts

  • A higher price for land-intensive grain raises land rent.
  • A tariff that raises the price of labour-intensive apparel raises the wage in the toy model.
  • The other factor’s real return moves in the opposite direction.

What evidence must add

  • Who owns land, labour, capital, and skills?
  • What do households consume and which firms use protected inputs?
  • How quickly can workers and capital adjust?
  • What are the tariff revenue, retaliation, and efficiency effects?

Discipline: use the model for a directional hypothesis, not as a complete tariff cost–benefit analysis.

AI changes which human tasks remain scarce

The H–O connection

Relative factor supplies and production techniques shape comparative advantage. AI and robots change the tasks machines can perform, so demand for different human capabilities changes.

Canadian export thought experiment: an auto plant uses fewer assembly hours per vehicle and expands exports. Maintenance and engineering can gain while some assembly tasks contract.

Three forces affect labour demand

  • Replacement: machines perform some human tasks.
  • Complementarity: machines make other human tasks more productive.
  • Expansion: lower costs can increase sales and exports.

Wages depend on the balance of these forces and on adjustment and institutions.

Model boundary: this is a technology extension. Stolper–Samuelson describes goods-price changes with technology fixed. More capital need not change wages in the diversified textbook economy at fixed world prices.

Task framework: Acemoglu and Restrepo, Automation and New Tasks (2019). The auto example is a thought experiment.

Concentrated losses can dominate political attention

Diffuse consumer gain

10,000,000 × $5 = $50M

Each consumer gains only $5, so few spend time organizing around the policy.

Concentrated producer loss

1,000 × $20,000 = $20M

Each affected worker or owner has a large stake and a strong reason to organize.

Net measured gain = $50M − $20M = $30M

Illustration only. Transition costs, taxes, uncertainty, and community spillovers are omitted.

Support for protection can be economically intelligible

Reasons for concern

  • One household or town can bear a large loss.
  • Specialized skills and assets cannot move quickly.
  • Housing, family obligations, credit, or licensing can constrain mobility.
  • Future market access and compensation remain uncertain.

What does not follow

  • Every tariff raises national income.
  • Protected firms will necessarily become competitive.
  • Downstream input costs are negligible.
  • Retaliation and lobbying can be ignored.

Understanding the demand for protection differs from concluding that protection is the best remedy.

ProblemMore targeted responseTest before acting

Benchmark: a restriction needs a diagnosed market or institutional failure and a measurable exit condition.

Model and evidence

  • Foreign restrictions reduce the Canadian producer price or quantity sold.
  • Slow-moving plants, equipment, and supplier networks can absorb the first loss.

  • Downstream users can face different effects from primary producers.
  • Distance limits rapid redirection to new markets.

What to remember

  1. H–O links relative factor abundance to the factor intensity of exports.
  2. Goods-price changes alter real factor returns; one factor can gain while another loses.
  3. H–O is a long-run benchmark; observed adjustment can still be sectoral, regional, and persistent when mobility is slow.
  4. Canadian trade liberalization combined productivity gains with concentrated adjustment.
  5. Long-run mobility can offset average losses while the transition remains economically important.
  6. Concentrated losses help explain support for protection without proving that protection is efficient.
  7. Adjustment and industrial policies need a diagnosed problem, evidence, and an exit test.
  8. AI can raise productivity while changing wages differently across tasks. Evidence must test who actually gains.

Core conclusion: gains from trade are more likely to remain politically durable when institutions address who bears the adjustment.
